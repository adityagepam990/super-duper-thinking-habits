# Critical Thinking Fallacies

## Most common logical fallacies at work:
1. Post Hoc, Ergo Propter Hoc ("after this, therefore because of this") Assuming that because one event followed another, the first caused the second — confusing correlation with causation. Example: Sales dropped after a new CEO was hired, so people assume the CEO caused the drop.

2. Ad Hominem Attacking the person making an argument instead of addressing the argument itself. Example: Dismissing someone's valid point about numbers not adding up simply because they lack a finance background.

3. Straw Man Misrepresenting or exaggerating someone's actual position, then attacking that distorted version instead of the real argument. Example: Twisting "open the job to candidates with less experience" into "eliminate all qualifications" to argue against it.

4. Hasty Generalization Drawing broad conclusions from a small, limited sample of evidence. Example: Citing a few famous college dropouts to claim that dropping out doesn't hurt your career.

## Key Takeaway: It's easy to notice these fallacies in people you disagree with — the real challenge (and true test of critical thinking) is identifying and eliminating them in your own reasoning.

## Avoiding the planning fallacy

* We tend to underestimate how long projects will take — a common reasoning flaw known as the planning fallacy.  This happens for two main reasons:

* Overoptimism — We fail to account for external factors (like economic disruptions) and internal factors (like team illness), and we find it easier to imagine success than the many ways a plan could fail.
* Ignoring past data — We assume our situation is unique, so we don't look at how similar projects have gone before.

## The Fix: Use the "Outside View"

* Most planning relies on the inside view — gathering input from contributors and projecting forward based on your own knowledge. Instead, critical thinking calls for the outside view: looking at how similar situations played out elsewhere and using that data as a realistic benchmark.

* Steps to apply the outside view:

* Identify a reference class — Find comparable past projects (e.g., past software upgrades, launches, or remodels). Focus on similarities, not differences — your situation likely isn't as unique as it feels.

* Gather statistics from that reference class — Look at data such as planned vs. actual timelines, budget overruns, average/most common outcomes, resource costs, and success/failure rates. Watch for outliers (extreme successes or failures) that could skew your forecast.

* Make or adjust your prediction — Use this reference data to build your plan, or to test and adjust a plan you already created using the inside view.

## Using Framing to your advantage:

1. Choose a Positive or Negative Frame
Positive frame: Focuses on why your option should win (e.g., "Acme should win" — highlighting best price, support, tech).
Negative frame: Focuses on why the alternative should lose (e.g., "Titan should lose" — highlighting its flaws).
The facts may be the same, but each frame directs attention differently — choose based on what you want people to focus on.
2. Choose a Gain Frame or Loss Frame
People are naturally more sensitive to losses than equivalent gains (loss aversion) — this is why most people reject a coin toss where they could lose $100 but gain $150.
3. When the outcome is clear/certain: Use a gain frame — it's more persuasive. Example: "You'll get favored vendor status if you ship on time."
4. When the outcome is uncertain: Use a loss frame — it's more persuasive. Example: "You won't get favored vendor status if you don't ship on time."
5. Match Emotional Intensity to Your Objective
Just like "smashed" felt faster than "hit" or "contacted," word choice can amplify or soften emotional impact.
6. Choose words that align with the reaction you want to evoke — 
e.g., describing disruptions as "massive" vs. "annoying" changes how serious they seem.

**Key takeaway**: Framing is a powerful persuasive tool — use it intentionally to strengthen your arguments, and stay alert to how others may be using it to influence your decisions.
