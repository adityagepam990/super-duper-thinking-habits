## Bucket Questions — Diversify Your Question Types

 Sort questions into three categories to avoid over-relying on one type:

1. Knowledge questions — answerable with evidence (e.g., "How many gaming consoles were sold in China last year?")
2. Opinion questions — subjective, not fully assessable (e.g., "Are multiplayer games more fun than single-player?")
3. Judgment questions — require reasoning/critical thinking (e.g., "Are Battle Royale games gaining or losing momentum?")
     * Example: Malik noticed his team asked too many opinion questions, so he pushed them to generate more knowledge and judgment questions for better balance.

## Interrogation Questions — Improve Question Quality

    Ask yourself these four checks:

1. Is it well-stated? — Make questions more specific (e.g., asking for a breakdown of gamers by platform, not just a total count).
2. Is it biased? — Watch for confirmation bias (e.g., "What shows the benefits of expanding?" vs. neutral "What does the data show?").
3. Does it reflect the issue's complexity? — Avoid oversimplified questions that miss critical nuances (e.g., China's content restrictions on supernatural/religious themes).
4. Is it relevant? — Make sure the question actually targets what matters (e.g., focusing on gamer demographics revealed the average Chinese gamer was older than assumed — more useful than income data).

**Key takeaway**: By bucketing your questions to diversify them and interrogating them to improve their quality, you create a virtuous circle — better questions fuel curiosity, and curiosity drives even better questions. The payoff: *stronger decisions, greater trust, and improved collaboration*.

## Preparing a Critical Thinking Workshop

* This piece outlines how to kick off a critical thinking workshop by getting participants to reflect not just on what they think, but how they think — and to sharpen that skill.

* Pre-Workshop Assignments (1 week before)

* Bring a "trigger song" (with earbuds) — playing it serves as a cue to shift from fast, intuitive thinking into slow, deliberate critical thinking.
Answer four competency questions:
* Q1.  What do I already know about critical thinking?
* Q2. How does it relate to something I already know?
* Q3. What questions should I have about it?
* Q4. What does the opposite of critical thinking look like?

## Three Key Mental Models to Jump-Start Critical Thinking

1. Circles of Competence This model helps create an honest picture of what you know vs. don't know. Warren Buffett famously used this to advise investors to stick to areas they truly understand — emphasizing that knowing the boundaries of your knowledge matters more than the size of the circle itself. The competency question answers can help map out your own "circle" for critical thinking (and later, for other topics too).

2. Inductive Reasoning Goes from specific → general: you observe something, notice a pattern, and form a broader theory. Example: "My boss got angry when challenged → anger signals stress → my boss is stressed."

3. Deductive Reasoning Goes from general → specific: you start with an established fact/theory, apply it to a specific case, and draw a logical conclusion. Example: "All anger is stress → my boss is angry → therefore my boss has stress."
*Pro tip*: Use inductive reasoning to form a theory, then use deductive reasoning to test whether it holds true — combining both strengthens overall reasoning skills.

**Takeaway**: Investing time and structure into critical thinking training pays off significantly — and a fun personal touch like trigger songs makes the exercise more memorable and engaging.
